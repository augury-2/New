@@TITLE From vision to measurable practice: circular accounting capability, chain transparency and the governance conditions of net-zero value creation in fragmented manufacturing networks

# Abstract

Net-zero manufacturing is a commitment expressed in quantities, yet the decisive share of those quantities arises outside the reporting boundary of the firm that commits to them. Circular economy strategies and smart production technologies are the principal levers for closing that distance, but neither is self-executing: digital instrumentation generates data, and data becomes a decision object only once it is quantified, costed and valued, while a valued opportunity becomes realised material re-routing only where some actor is answerable for the loss. In fragmented manufacturing networks of dispersed small producers, informal intermediaries and specialised processors, conventional management accounting recognises purchased inputs and saleable outputs while the fraction leaving a production process as waste is recognised only as an absence. This study operationalises circular accounting capability as a measurable, actor-level construct spanning organisational boundaries, and specifies a moderated serial-mediation model in which the capability raises circular information transparency, transparency enables circular value-chain integration conditional on circular governance quality, and integration delivers economic, environmental and social circular value creation. The framework combines the dynamic capabilities view with value-chain governance theory and a socio-technical reading of smart production, treating governance as the complementary asset that substitutes for hierarchy where the value-creating reconfiguration crosses firm boundaries. Three rounds of expert validation with an eight-member panel raised scale-level content validity from 0.799 to 0.908 and established the construct's discriminant boundaries, with leakage between the accounting–transparency and governance–integration pairs of 4.2 and 2.1 per cent against a pre-specified 20 per cent tolerance. Formal specification analysis rejects the intuitive four-link serial chain, whose indirect effect falls below 0.03 once path coefficients drop under 0.40. The paper delivers a validated instrument and a pre-registrable estimation protocol for the measurement and accountability infrastructure on which industrial decarbonisation depends.

# Keywords

circular economy; net-zero manufacturing; circular accounting capability; supply chain transparency; value-chain governance; smart production; sustainable operations; scale development

# 1. Introduction

Net-zero manufacturing is, before it is anything else, an accounting proposition. A commitment to carbon neutrality is a commitment about quantities: how much material a production system retains, how much it loses, how much embodied carbon leaves with the loss, and how much of what is recovered is captured as economic, environmental and social value. Circular economy strategies are the principal physical lever for moving those quantities, and smart production technologies are the principal instrumentation for observing them (Chauhan, Parida, and Dhir 2022; Acerbi, Sassanelli, and Taisch 2024). The distance between the vision and the practice, however, is not exhausted by technology or by ambition. It is occupied by a calculative and an accountability problem, and the two are related in a way that has not been specified or tested.

The calculative problem is that the accounting systems which would supply the decisive quantities were built for linear flows. They recognise purchased inputs and saleable outputs; the fraction leaving a process as waste is recognised only as an absence, a residual that the cost system distributes over good output rather than an object with a quantity, a cost and a forgone value. Loop-closing decisions in production — whether to recover a by-product, to invest in reprocessing capacity, to collect a discarded fraction, to redirect an off-grade consignment — presuppose exactly the quantities that the conventional system does not compute. Material flow cost accounting was developed to correct this within a process boundary (Christ and Burritt 2015; Wang, Tang, and Shen 2023), and product-level carbon accounting has advanced considerably in allocating organisational emissions to manufactured items (He, Qian, and Li 2023; Xiang et al. 2024). Both remain bounded by the legal entity or the plant. What is not measured cannot be managed, governed or assured, and in a production network the most consequential unmeasured quantities sit between firms rather than inside them.

The accountability problem is that measurement does not act. This is where the fragmentation of contemporary manufacturing becomes decisive. In a vertically integrated firm, sensing and acting are joined: information about a recoverable flow reaches a decision-maker with the authority to redirect resources. In a fragmented network they are not. The producer who can observe how much of a batch is rejected has no processing capacity; the processor who could valorise that material cannot see it; the intermediary between them has an interest in neither party seeing the other's prices. Nobody owns the loop, and no hierarchy coordinates its closure. Fragmentation is neither peripheral nor a feature of developing economies. Closing a resource loop characteristically requires coordination among actors with no prior exchange relationship (De Angelis, Howard, and Miemczyk 2018; Farooque et al. 2019b), and the evidence that coordination rather than technology is the binding constraint is drawn overwhelmingly from advanced industrial settings: supply chain redesign in European manufacturing (Bressanelli, Perona, and Saccani 2019), Dutch circular business models (Vermunt et al. 2019), the built environment, which is the European Union's largest waste stream, where dispersed responsibility across many small contractors is the recurring obstacle (Adams et al. 2017; Hart et al. 2019), and circular networks that self-organise because no incumbent actor can coordinate loop closure unaided (Massari, Nacchiero, and Giannoccaro 2025; Ratsimandresy and Miemczyk 2024). Circular manufacturing archetypes remain without a template (Batista et al. 2018; Zils, Howard, and Hopkinson 2023), and novel intermediaries emerge precisely to supply the missing coordination (Ubakivi-Hadachi and Kall 2026). Wherever a recoverable flow crosses many hands and no actor controls it, the same structural problem obtains.

## 1.1 The smart production paradox

The obvious response to a calculative gap is instrumentation, and this is the response that industrial policy and much of the operations literature has adopted. Digital product passports, industrial internet-of-things sensing, blockchain-anchored traceability and data-driven circular maturity models all promise to make material flows legible across production networks (Patro et al. 2025; Agyemang et al. 2026; Gosling et al. 2026). Yet the empirical record is markedly less confident than the policy rhetoric. Reviews of digitalisation and circularity find the link asserted more often than demonstrated (Chauhan, Parida, and Dhir 2022); studies of smart technology adoption for circular ends report drivers and barriers rather than realised performance (Truant et al. 2024); digital transformation in small and medium-sized manufacturers produces unintended consequences for circularity as readily as intended ones (Chung, Tan, and Yoshie 2025); and transparency itself has been shown to operate paradoxically, improving some outcomes while degrading others (Wang, Prajogo, and Gligor 2026b). Where digital transformation does reduce carbon, it appears to do so through capability development rather than directly (Yang et al. 2023; Belhadi et al. 2024).

This pattern is intelligible once two distinct conversions are separated. Digital technologies generate data; they do not generate economic objects. A sensor stream recording rejected volume is not a costed loss, and a costed loss is not a valued recovery opportunity comparable against an alternative use of the same capacity. The first conversion, from data to decision-relevant quantity, is accounting work. The second conversion, from a visible opportunity to coordinated material re-routing across firms that do not share a hierarchy, is governance work. Smart production supplies the substrate for both and accomplishes neither. Treating digital adoption as sufficient collapses a three-stage mechanism into one, which is a plausible explanation for the instability of reported effects.

## 1.2 Research gap

Three gaps follow, and they compound.

First, the calculative capability itself has been named but never operationalised. A nascent literature conceptualises circular accounting as a dynamic capability (Scarpellini et al. 2020; Aranda-Usón, Scarpellini, and Moneva 2024) and case evidence establishes that existing management accounting systems cannot support circular decisions (Aureli, Foschi, and Paletta 2025). Because the construct has no measurable, actor-level specification, its consequences for circular and carbon outcomes cannot be estimated. Instrument development for circular practice has advanced at the level of adoption barriers (Mishra, Singh, and Govindan 2022) and organisational capability inventories (Bezerra and Gohr 2026), but not for the calculative capacity that must precede either.

Second, although several literatures assert that information must be verified, contested or owed before it changes behaviour, none specifies governance as a conditional mechanism converting circular visibility into joint action, and none has estimated it. Verification improves sustainability outcomes by aligning accountability rather than by adding information (Gualandris et al. 2015); disclosure without verification or consequence is inert (Sodhi and Tang 2019); visibility affects responsiveness only in interaction with integrating mechanisms (Williams et al. 2013); power and performance-measurement arrangements determine whether sustainability requirements cascade through multi-tier production networks at all (Marttinen and Kähkönen 2022; Saunila, Ukko, and Jääskeläinen 2024). The conditional logic is everywhere present and nowhere modelled.

Third, circular and net-zero performance in production research is predominantly measured as material-flow or energy efficiency, leaving circular value creation — economic capture from recovery, environmental and embodied-carbon loss reduction, and equitable social distribution — without an integrated measure (Corona et al. 2019; Kristensen and Mosgaard 2020; Jerome et al. 2022; de Oliveira and Oliveira 2023). The social dimension is the least instrumented of the three (Pitkänen et al. 2023; Blandino and Montagna 2025), which matters because the distributional question in a fragmented network is precisely who captures the value that circularity releases.

The accounting and governance mechanisms through which circular resource flows in fragmented manufacturing networks become measurable, visible and actionable therefore remain insufficiently theorised and untested. This paper develops a theory, operationalises its central construct, validates the instrument, and specifies its test, asking:

- RQ1. Does circular accounting capability increase circular information transparency across the actors of a fragmented manufacturing network?
- RQ2. Does circular information transparency translate into circular value-chain integration, and is that translation conditional on circular governance quality?
- RQ3. Through what indirect pathways does circular accounting capability affect economic, environmental and social circular value creation?

## 1.3 Contributions and scope

The paper makes four contributions. It operationalises circular accounting capability as a three-dimensional construct measurable at the individual network actor and across organisational boundaries, and reports three rounds of expert validation in full, including where they fall short. It extends the dynamic capabilities view by identifying what substitutes for hierarchy when a capability's returns depend on coordination the focal actor does not control, which is the standing condition of net-zero targets that reach beyond the factory gate. It makes falsifiable the claim, widely asserted in the transparency and disclosure literatures, that governance is a conversion mechanism rather than an additive good. And it delivers a pre-registrable protocol, including a formal comparison of specification alternatives and a computed sample-size requirement, so that the theory can be tested rather than asserted.

Two boundaries are stated at the outset. The structural model is specified and its instrument validated here; it is not estimated, and no structural results are reported. The empirical content of this paper is the construct development and the three-round expert validation, and every claim about the mechanism is a prediction with a stated failure condition rather than a finding. Second, the framework applies under fragmentation. Where hierarchy performs the coordinating work the conditional effect should attenuate toward zero, which makes the scope condition testable rather than merely asserted.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on net-zero and circular manufacturing, the calculative gap, smart production, transparency and governance, and locates the contribution against the closest prior work. Section 3 develops the theoretical framework. Section 4 derives nine hypotheses, compares specification alternatives and states failure conditions. Sections 5 and 6 set out the research design and the analytical protocol, and report the expert validation results. Sections 7 to 11 discuss the findings and their theoretical, managerial, sustainability and policy implications. Section 12 states limitations and a research agenda, and Section 13 concludes.

# 2. Literature review

## 2.1 Net-zero manufacturing and circular production: from commitment to computable practice

Industrial decarbonisation has moved from aspiration to obligation, and production research has responded with a rapidly maturing body of work on the pathways available to manufacturing systems. Strategic reviews map routes to carbon neutrality across manufacturing supply chains (Vimal et al. 2026) and identify net-zero capabilities as the organisational condition for low-carbon operations in circular chains (Awan, Shamim, and Hannola 2025). Sector studies quantify what material and technological efficiency can deliver in hard-to-abate production (Garvey, Norman, and Barrett 2022), and process-level work demonstrates carbon reduction through the intersection of value chains, product redesign and the de-manufacturing of industrial scrap (Cappelletti and Germani 2023). At network level, low-carbon manufacturing has been framed as an integrated process rather than a technology portfolio (Hu, Liu, and Shi 2025), and smart platforming has been shown to couple intelligentisation, green technology and innovation dynamics in automotive production oriented to net zero (Zhang et al. 2024). Analytical work examines the instruments: carbon taxes shaping the transition from linear to circular business models in manufacturing (Panza and Peron 2025), cap-and-trade and border adjustment in cross-regional production (Hua et al. 2024), and green finance conditioning innovation efficiency along two-stage value chains (Liu et al. 2026). Reviews of the analytical toolkit itself find big data analytics applied to decarbonisation to be an emerging rather than a settled capability (Kumar et al. 2023).

Two features of this literature matter for the present argument. The first is that the great majority of the abatement potential it identifies lies upstream or downstream of the focal plant, in purchased materials, in losses at handover points, and in recovery that requires a partner. The second is that its quantities are assumed to be available. Whether a firm can compute the loss at its own boundary, let alone the loss occurring at a boundary it does not control, is treated as an implementation detail rather than as a research object. Empirical work on ESG and emission reduction in manufacturing firms is beginning to expose the consequences, finding that uncertainty in the measurement and evaluation regime itself dampens abatement (Yin et al. 2026a).

The circular economy supplies the strategic vocabulary for the material side of the same problem. The concept stabilised through definitional consolidation (Kirchherr, Reike, and Hekkert 2017; Kirchherr et al. 2023) around resource-loop closure, while diverging on whether circularity implies sustainable development (Geissdoerfer et al. 2017). Bocken et al. (2016) supplied the vocabulary of slowing, closing and narrowing loops, and Velenturf and Purnell (2021) insisted that the object of circular management is value, not merely material. In production and operations research, circularity has been examined at scheduling level (Castiglione, Alfieri, and Pastore 2025), in supplier selection (Bai, Zhu, and Sarkis 2022), in implementation pathways for operations and supply chain transformation (Zils, Howard, and Hopkinson 2023), in remanufacturing-based circular chains (Werner-Lewandowska, Golinska-Dawson, and Mierzwiak 2025), and in the resilience of planned as against self-organised circular networks (Massari, Nacchiero, and Giannoccaro 2025). Corvellec, Stowell, and Johansson (2022) find the wider literature under-theorised and inclined to attribute agency to "the circular economy" rather than to identifiable actors and mechanisms, a criticism answered by naming actors and estimating mechanisms, which is what a structural model does.

The measurement literature makes the accounting problem concrete. Corona et al. (2019) find circularity metrics dominated by physical indicators poorly connected to economic value and largely silent on social outcomes; Kristensen and Mosgaard (2020) concur for firm-level indicators; product-level indicators are inconsistently specified and rarely tested (Jerome et al. 2022); and reviews of what circularity indicators actually measure find weak correspondence with the Sustainable Development Goals they are invoked to serve (de Oliveira and Oliveira 2023). Indicator selection in agri-industrial settings remains contested (Poponi et al. 2022), and corporate-supply-chain circularity indicators require substantial technical qualification before they support decisions (Halter et al. 2025). Kirchherr et al. (2018) show the binding barriers in the European Union to be cultural, market and regulatory rather than technological, with weak information and unclear responsibility prominent among them, a finding replicated for small and medium-sized manufacturers (Dey et al. 2022; Mishra, Singh, and Govindan 2022) and for agro-industrial production in emerging economies (Abdul-Hamid et al. 2022). Circularity in production is constrained less by what is technically possible than by what is calculated and who is answerable for it.

## 2.2 Accounting for circular and carbon flows in production networks

Accounting's engagement with sustainability was framed by Burritt and Schaltegger (2010), who distinguished managerial from reporting-driven trajectories, and by Bebbington and Larrinaga (2014), who argued that sustainable development requires accounting to engage with systemic, boundary-crossing rather than entity-bounded phenomena. That argument licenses treating a production network, not a firm, as the accounting domain, and it is the premise of the present study.

Environmental management accounting supplies the closest instrumentation. Ferreira, Moulang, and Hendro (2010) developed a survey measure of its use and linked it to innovation; Gunarathne and Lee (2015) traced its role in organisational change; and Gunarathne, Lee, and Hitigala Kaluarachchilage (2021) modelled institutional pressures, strategy and performance. Material flow cost accounting is the technique most obviously aligned with circular production: Christ and Burritt (2015, 2017) reviewed it and applied it to food waste, Tran et al. (2025) documented how organisational and institutional conditions shape its use, and recent applications couple it explicitly to carbon analysis in materials recovery (Wang, Tang, and Shen 2023). Henri and Journeault (2010) established that eco-controls improve environmental and, conditionally, economic performance — an early demonstration of precisely the contingent relationship this paper generalises. On the carbon side, methods now exist to allocate multi-level organisational emissions data to individual manufactured products (Xiang et al. 2024) and to model product carbon footprints at process level (He, Qian, and Li 2023), and blockchain-anchored architectures have been proposed to make footprint accounting traceable and auditable across an industry (Patro et al. 2025).

Circular-economy-specific accounting work is recent and largely qualitative. Scarpellini et al. (2020) showed that environmental accounting practices function as dynamic capabilities shaping firms' circular scope; Aranda-Usón, Scarpellini, and Moneva (2024) developed the concept of circular accounting for material flows; Scarpellini, Kuba-Khoury, and Aranda-Usón (2026) extended it to a stakeholder frame; and Aureli, Foschi, and Paletta (2025) concluded that existing management accounting systems cannot support circular decisions. On the reporting side, Opferkuch et al. (2021, 2022) document fragmented circular disclosure, Massari and Giannoccaro (2023) analyse standards-based disclosure, Radu and Lux (2025) examine the public sector, and Johnstone (2024) analyses digital data enabling circular control in an international manufacturing firm.

Two features define the opportunity. The literature is overwhelmingly case-based or content-analytic rather than explanatory of production outcomes; and where survey instruments exist they measure firm-internal environmental accounting, not accounting across an inter-organisational loop. Circular accounting is therefore a concept awaiting operationalisation and test. The concept is not claimed as novel — it is that of Aranda-Usón, Scarpellini, and Moneva (2024) — but it is operationalised here, moved across the organisational boundary, and placed in an estimable model.

## 2.3 Smart production, digital traceability and the data-to-decision gap

Smart production is the technological substrate on which circular measurement in manufacturing is now expected to rest, and the literature on the coupling is large and increasingly cautious. Systematic reviews establish that the circularity–digitalisation link is more often asserted than demonstrated (Chauhan, Parida, and Dhir 2022), and that the mechanisms by which smart technologies deliver circular performance remain under-specified (Truant et al. 2024). Capability-based accounts have made the most progress: integrating circular economy and Industry 4.0 for sustainable supply chain management has been theorised through the dynamic capability view (Lu, Zhao, and Liu 2022), digital-enabled dynamic capabilities for circularity have been shown to operate differently by firm size (Urueña et al. 2026), and data-driven circular manufacturing has been given a maturity structure that treats data infrastructure as necessary but insufficient (Acerbi, Sassanelli, and Taisch 2024). Related work links Industry 4.0 integration into green supply chains to green innovation and circular outcomes (Shahzad, Ben Zaied, and Zhang 2026), examines digital innovation among international industrial manufacturers pursuing circularity (Oghazi, Mostaghel, and Hultman 2024), and documents unintended consequences of digital transformation for circularity in small and medium-sized manufacturers (Chung, Tan, and Yoshie 2025). Digital transformation's carbon effect appears to run through dynamic capability rather than directly (Yang et al. 2023), and data-driven transformation for carbon neutrality has been shown to depend on cross-sector coordination arrangements (Belhadi et al. 2024). Artificial intelligence is being examined as an accelerator of supply chain carbon reduction (Yin et al. 2026b; Zheng et al. 2025; Wu 2026), with capability frameworks emerging for generative systems in operations (Jackson et al. 2024).

Policy instruments have advanced faster than the evidence. The digital product passport is the clearest case: envisioned as the informational backbone of circular supply chains in the built environment (Gosling et al. 2026) and analysed as an ecosystem-orchestration problem in practice (Agyemang et al. 2026). The orchestration framing is the important one. A passport is a data structure. Whether the data it carries becomes a costed loss, a valued recovery opportunity and an allocated responsibility depends on calculative and governance arrangements that the instrument does not itself supply. This paper's contribution to that debate is to name the two missing conversions and to model them.

## 2.4 Transparency, visibility and traceability

Operations research supplies the instrumentation that accounting lacks. Barratt and Oke (2007) theorised visibility from a resource-based perspective, its value depending on how it is generated; Williams et al. (2013) supplied a validated measure and found its effect on responsiveness moderated by internal integration, which is the clearest precedent for a conditional visibility effect; Li et al. (2005) validated instruments for information sharing and quality. Theoretical consolidation of supply chain transparency has followed (Morgan, Gabler, and Manhart 2023), alongside intervention-based evidence that transparency for sustainability must be built rather than disclosed (Adhi Santharm and Ramanathan 2022), multi-tier case evidence on the practical limits of traceability (Schaëfer, Kähkönen, and Luzzini 2025), hierarchical assessment of transparency practices in semiconductor production (Bui 2024), and analyses of when full transparency is preferable to restricted visibility in blockchain-enabled data sharing (He et al. 2025). Carbon-specific transparency has been examined directly, with evidence that disclosing carbon information to consumers can harm as well as help (Zhu, Duan, and Sarkis 2023), and decarbonisation's financial returns have been shown to depend jointly on supply chain transparency and resilience (Wang et al. 2026a). In fragmented agri-industrial settings, information sharing is constrained by exactly the intermediary structures this paper studies (Obonyo, Formentini, and Ndiritu 2025; Zhou, Lu, and Xu 2023), and traceability has been reframed under a circular lens (Kersten et al. 2024) and indexed for governance purposes (Makarenko, Steiner, and Yuhai 2024).

On transparency's limits, Sodhi and Tang (2019) observe that disclosure without verification or consequence changes nothing, and Gualandris et al. (2015) show that verification works by aligning accountability. The paradoxical findings are now numerous enough to be systematic: transparency simultaneously enables and undermines (Wang, Prajogo, and Gligor 2026b). This literature treats transparency as an output of information systems and an input to trust or responsiveness. It is never connected upstream to the accounting capability that makes the information exist, nor conditioned downstream on governance quality within one estimated model, and its measures concern commercial rather than circular and carbon information.

## 2.5 Governance of production networks and of circularity

Gereffi, Humphrey, and Sturgeon (2005) derive value-chain governance form from transaction complexity, the codifiability of information, and supplier capability. Two implications matter here. Codifiability is constitutive of governance form and accounting is the technology that raises it, which makes the accounting–governance link implicit in the theory. And the "captive" configuration — dispersed suppliers depending on concentrated intermediaries under low codifiability — describes fragmented manufacturing networks precisely.

For circularity, Farooque et al. (2019b) show that closing loops requires coordination between previously unconnected actors, and Farooque, Zhang, and Liu (2019a) find fragmented responsibility a leading barrier in circular food production. De Angelis, Howard, and Miemczyk (2018) and Batista et al. (2018) argue that circular chains demand governance arrangements linear chains do not supply. Tong et al. (2024) analyse extended producer responsibility as a device for reallocating responsibility, and analytical work examines its production consequences under servitisation (Jin et al. 2024). The multi-tier sustainability literature has converged on governance as the operative variable: power sources determine whether sustainability requirements cascade at all (Marttinen and Kähkönen 2022); institutional entrepreneurship shapes standard diffusion in multi-tier production (Grimm, Hofstetter, and Sarkis 2022); relational capital raises sustainability through governance, and the effect is moderated by network complexity (Chowdhury et al. 2022); performance measurement and management is the operative governance mechanism for sustainability among small and medium-sized suppliers (Saunila, Ukko, and Jääskeläinen 2024); accountability failures propagate where responsibility is diffuse (Uddin, Ahmed, and Shahadat 2023); and upstream agency and conflicting institutional logics determine governance outcomes in agricultural production networks (Khan et al. 2026). Multi-tier sustainability outcomes in manufacturing have been estimated directly (Durak Uşar and Soytas 2022; Oyedijo et al. 2023; Kusi-Sarpong et al. 2023). In agricultural production settings, Österberg and Nilsson (2009) provide a validated measure of perceived participation in cooperative governance, and Gurung, Choubey, and Rai (2024) examine producer organisations as governance vehicles.

Governance is thus theorised structurally, as a type of network architecture, or institutionally, as policy, and rarely as a measurable actor-level property positionable in a variance model. Where it is measured in agricultural settings it concerns generic cooperative participation, not responsibility for losses and recovery.

## 2.6 Circular value creation and the empirical modelling stream

Flynn, Huo, and Zhao (2010) is the benchmark for supply chain integration and performance; Cao and Zhang (2011) validated collaboration instruments; Zhu, Sarkis, and Lai (2008) confirmed a measurement model for green supply chain practices including investment recovery. Quantitative circular-economy work in production research has grown quickly. Bag et al. (2020), Osei, Asante-Darko, and Quayson (2024) and Forliano et al. (2026) link analytics, stakeholder pressure and institutional context to circular practice and performance; adoption among small and medium-sized manufacturers has been estimated across Europe (Dey et al. 2022) and modelled through extended institutional theory in seafood processing (Do et al. 2022); performance measurement frameworks for circular chain implementation have been compared across cases (Lahane et al. 2023); circular economy and blockchain adoption have been related to financial performance (Sun, Bai, and Sarkis 2025); and digitalisation has been positioned as a moderator between supply chain efficiency and circular performance (Wang et al. 2026c), which is the closest published analogue to the conditional logic advanced here. Capability inventories for circularity have been proposed and validated (Bezerra and Gohr 2026), and knowledge obstacles in circular transition have been documented intra-organisationally (Nujen et al. 2023).

The closest comparator remains Kristoffersen et al. (2021), who model business analytics capability affecting circular economy implementation and firm performance through resource orchestration. Its logic — that an information capability requires an orchestrating mechanism — is the nearest structural analogue and must be credited. The present model differs on four axes: accounting rather than analytics; network rather than firm level; orchestration as contingency rather than mediator; and circular value creation rather than generic performance.

# 3. Theoretical framework

## 3.1 Selection of theory

Candidate theories were assessed against five requirements: to explain why an accounting capability is a capability rather than a practice; why measurement precedes cross-actor visibility; why visibility does not automatically produce joint action; why governance supplies the missing condition; and why the outcome is multi-dimensional.

The dynamic capabilities view is adopted as the primary lens, with value-chain governance theory as its principal complement and socio-technical systems theory supplying the framing that connects the model to the smart-production agenda. The dynamic capabilities view is also the lens under which the closest circular-and-digital work in production research has converged (Lu, Zhao, and Liu 2022; Yang et al. 2023; Urueña et al. 2026; Awan, Shamim, and Hannola 2025), which makes the theoretical choice continuous with the field rather than idiosyncratic to it.

Five alternatives are declined for substantive reasons, and the reasons are stated because a declined theory is an argument rather than an omission. Institutional theory explains adoption rather than value creation, and its central antecedent — regulatory pressure for circularity — has almost no variance in the proposed setting, since circularity regulation, extended producer responsibility and mandatory disclosure typically do not reach these actors; institutional conditions are modelled as controls instead. This is a deliberate divergence from the seafood-processing application of extended institutional theory (Do et al. 2022), where regulatory reach is the operative variable. Stakeholder theory informs the multi-dimensional specification of circular value but generates no directional predictions among capabilities. The natural-resource-based view is the most tempting candidate given the subject matter, and it is declined on a precise ground: it locates the resource and its rent inside the firm, and cannot accommodate a capability whose returns depend on an inter-organisational arrangement the focal actor does not own. The technology–organisation–environment framework is a well-established lens for digital adoption but explains adoption rather than the conversion of adopted technology into value, which is the present object. Resource dependence theory explains why fragmented networks remain information-poor, opacity sustaining intermediary power, and motivates the network-position controls, but is silent on calculability. Contingency theory supplies the logical form of the moderation hypothesis and is used as such, but is a grammar rather than a substantive theory of what the variables are.

## 3.2 Mapping capabilities onto the mechanism

Teece's (2007) decomposition maps onto the model with unusual directness, which is the principal reason for its selection.

*Sensing* is the identification of opportunity, and circular opportunities in production do not announce themselves. A discarded fraction, an off-grade consignment, an unrecovered by-product, the embodied carbon leaving with each: none is an opportunity until quantified, valued and made comparable against an alternative use of the same capacity. Accounting is the sensing apparatus of circularity, which is why circular accounting capability enters as an antecedent rather than a practice. Smart production instrumentation extends the reach of sensing but does not constitute it; a sensor stream is a signal, and a costed loss is an opportunity.

*Seizing* requires mobilising resources and diffusing sensed information to those who must act. Within a firm this is internal; in a fragmented network whoever senses a loss is rarely whoever can seize the recovery opportunity, so calculability must become cross-actor visibility. Circular information transparency is the inter-organisational form of seizing capacity.

*Transforming* is the recombination of assets and routines. Closing a loop is reconfiguration: material that previously exited the network is redirected, requiring joint planning, shared assets and coordinated timing. Circular value-chain integration is reconfiguration at network level, and the mechanism through which circular and carbon value is realised.

## 3.3 Why capability is insufficient, and what supplies the difference

Dynamic capabilities theory is not linear, a point frequently lost in capability-and-performance research. Teece (2007) is explicit that capabilities yield value only alongside co-specialised complementary assets and effective orchestration, and Eisenhardt and Martin (2000) likewise treat capabilities as necessary but not sufficient. This generates the paper's central argument as theory rather than as an ad hoc interaction:

> Circular information transparency is a capability output whose conversion into reconfiguration depends on an orchestrating complementary asset. In a single firm, hierarchy performs the orchestration. In a fragmented production network there is no hierarchy, so orchestration must be supplied by governance.

Value-chain governance theory specifies what governance does: it allocates responsibility, monitors conformance and enforces commitments. In a captive configuration with no codified loss standards, no actor holds responsibility for material leaving the network as waste. Loss is nobody's account, and transparency about it therefore changes nothing, because visibility without an accountability relation creates no obligation to act. Where responsibility is allocated, visibility identifies whose problem the loss is. Where monitoring exists, the information is credible rather than asserted. Where decisions are participatory, actors can negotiate how recovered value is shared, absent which transparency may even reduce cooperation by exposing inequity — the mechanism behind the paradoxical transparency findings reported by Zhu, Duan, and Sarkis (2023) and Wang, Prajogo, and Gligor (2026b). And where commitments are enforceable, an actor can invest in recovery assets without fearing hold-up. The recent multi-tier evidence supports each link: power determines cascade (Marttinen and Kähkönen 2022), measurement systems operationalise governance for smaller suppliers (Saunila, Ukko, and Jääskeläinen 2024), and relational governance effects are themselves contingent on network structure (Chowdhury et al. 2022).

## 3.4 A socio-technical reading, and its relevance to smart production

The argument has a natural socio-technical statement, and making it explicit connects the model to the operations literature on Industry 4.0 and Industry 5.0 implementation. Socio-technical systems theory holds that performance depends on the joint optimisation of a technical and a social subsystem, and that intervening on one alone produces the disappointing or paradoxical outcomes that digitalisation research repeatedly reports (Colombari, Neirotti, and Berbegal-Mirabent 2024; Qamar et al. 2026; Cammarano and Varriale 2026; Zhang et al. 2026). In this model the technical subsystem comprises the digital instrumentation and the calculative apparatus that converts its output into economic and carbon objects — smart production plus circular accounting capability, jointly producing transparency. The social subsystem is the accountability architecture: allocated responsibility, joint monitoring, participatory decision-making, enforceable commitment. The prediction that follows is precisely the focal hypothesis: investment in the technical subsystem alone will not deliver circular reconfiguration, because the conversion is performed by the social subsystem. This explains why digital product passports, traceability mandates and Industry 4.0 investment programmes generate compliance data at scale while circular outcomes remain uneven, and it supplies a testable form for a claim that the socio-technical literature has largely made qualitatively.

Consistent with this reading, digital traceability capability is retained as a measured control rather than promoted to a structural path. The theoretical claim is not that digital technology is irrelevant — it is that digital technology is a substrate whose effect on circular value is mediated by calculation and conditioned by accountability. Modelling it as a rival explanation and demonstrating that the accounting mechanism survives its inclusion is a stronger test of that claim than adding it as a second predictor. Section 12 sets out how future work should relax this restriction.

# 4. Hypothesis development

Nine hypotheses follow, corresponding to the paths in Figure 1. Two are framed to permit disconfirmation, and the framework's failure conditions are stated explicitly in Section 4.4. Each hypothesis is stated, then justified theoretically, then related to the production and net-zero outcome it is intended to explain.

## 4.1 The mechanism

**H1. Circular accounting capability is positively associated with circular information transparency.** Information cannot be shared before it exists. Where an actor does not quantify rejected volumes, cost spoilage, or estimate the value of an unrecovered by-product, there is no circular information to make visible however willing that actor may be to share. Accounting also codifies, converting tacit awareness that "a lot goes to waste" into transmissible form, and codifiability is what permits cross-boundary coordination (Gereffi, Humphrey, and Sturgeon 2005; Christ and Burritt 2015; Aranda-Usón, Scarpellini, and Moneva 2024). The same logic underlies the carbon case: an emission becomes shareable only once allocated to a physical flow (Xiang et al. 2024). For net-zero manufacturing the implication is direct — the Scope 3 quantities on which chain-level targets depend are constructed by actor-level calculative capacity, not discovered by disclosure mandates.

**H2. Circular information transparency is positively associated with circular value-chain integration.** Coordinated recovery in a production network requires actors to know what material exists, in what quantity and condition, when, and at what value. Where losses are invisible and downstream prices unknown, partners cannot agree quantities, schedules or shares of a flow they cannot jointly observe (Li et al. 2005; Williams et al. 2013; Kersten et al. 2024; Schaëfer, Kähkönen, and Luzzini 2025). Transparency is the precondition for the joint planning that circular manufacturing requires but linear scheduling does not (Castiglione, Alfieri, and Pastore 2025).

**H3. Circular value-chain integration is positively associated with circular value creation performance.** Circular and carbon value is realised only through changed material routing, and routing is a joint accomplishment: an actor cannot unilaterally valorise a by-product for which it has no processing capacity, offtake or logistics. Recovery requires aggregation to viable scale, timing against perishability or degradation, and shared assets, and it generates economic, environmental and social value together (Flynn, Huo, and Zhao 2010; Zhu, Sarkis, and Lai 2008; Farooque et al. 2019b; Zils, Howard, and Hopkinson 2023). Process-level evidence from manufacturing confirms that abatement of this kind is achieved at the intersection of value chains rather than within a plant (Cappelletti and Germani 2023).

**H4. Circular accounting capability is positively associated with circular value-chain integration, over and above its indirect effect through transparency.** Not every route from measurement to joint action passes through network-wide visibility. An actor that has appraised a recovery opportunity gains bargaining and initiating capacity: it can propose an arrangement, quantify the mutual gain and negotiate a share without the information becoming generally visible. This is the mechanism by which a well-instrumented processor initiates a recovery arrangement that no passport or platform disclosed.

**H5. The direct association between circular information transparency and circular value creation performance is weak or non-significant once integration is accounted for.** This hypothesis is framed to be disconfirmed. The claim is that information does not create circular value; changed material routing does. A strong direct effect net of integration would favour a simpler information-economics explanation — transparency improves bargaining, prices rise, and value is captured with no change in material flows — over the mediated conditional account (Sodhi and Tang 2019; Aureli, Foschi, and Paletta 2025). The distinction matters for net-zero policy, because a transparency instrument that raises captured value without altering physical flows delivers no abatement.

**H6a. Circular governance quality is positively associated with circular value-chain integration.** Clear responsibility, joint monitoring, participatory decision-making and enforceable commitment lower the safeguarding costs of cooperation, making actors willing to commit dedicated assets to recovery activities they would otherwise avoid (Marttinen and Kähkönen 2022; Saunila, Ukko, and Jääskeläinen 2024).

**H6b (focal). Circular governance quality positively moderates the relationship between circular information transparency and circular value-chain integration, such that the relationship is stronger where governance quality is higher.** The argument is developed in Section 3.3. Where governance is weak the same information is inert or counterproductive: it reveals an opportunity that no actor is empowered, obliged or safe to act upon. Supporting evidence is scattered across literatures but has never been estimated as a moderation: Gualandris et al. (2015) on verification aligning accountability; Sodhi and Tang (2019) on inert transparency; Aureli, Foschi, and Paletta (2025) on participatory process alongside accounting information; Williams et al. (2013) on conditional visibility; Kristoffersen et al. (2021) on capability requiring orchestration; Chowdhury et al. (2022) and Wang et al. (2026c) on structural and digital contingencies in comparable models; Agyemang et al. (2026) on passports requiring ecosystem orchestration. The predicted form is illustrated in Figure 2.

## 4.2 Indirect and conditional effects

**H7.** Circular information transparency mediates the relationship between circular accounting capability and circular value-chain integration; complementary (partial) mediation is expected given H4.

**H8.** Circular value-chain integration mediates the relationship between transparency and circular value creation; indirect-only (full) mediation is expected given H5.

**H9.** Transparency and integration serially mediate the relationship between circular accounting capability and circular value creation. Because no direct path from capability to value creation is specified, the claim is clean: the effect of accounting on circular value creation is entirely indirect. Accounting does not create value; it makes value creation organisable. Because governance moderates a path inside the mediated chain, that indirect effect is itself conditional, so an index of moderated mediation is required, and the conditional indirect effect is the study's central empirical quantity.

## 4.3 Specification analysis: why this model and not the obvious alternatives

Three candidate specifications were developed and compared (Figure 3).

@@FIG image3.png|Figure 3. The three candidate specifications. Models 1 and 2 are rejected; Model 3 is adopted. The rejected models are the ones a reader would otherwise propose. Source: Author(s).

Alternative 1, the fully serial chain (accounting → transparency → governance → integration → value), is intuitively appealing and is rejected on three independent grounds. The path from transparency to governance is directionally indefensible in cross-section, for the reasons given in Section 3.3. The four-link indirect effect is statistically self-defeating: as a product of standardised paths it is two to three-and-a-half times smaller than the three-link alternative at every value, being 0.063 against 0.125 where each link is 0.50 and 0.008 against 0.027 where each is 0.30, and falling below 0.03 once paths drop under 0.40, as Table 3 sets out. Across most of the plausible range, therefore, the headline claim would rest on a quantity too small to discuss. And two adjacent construct pairs — accounting capability with transparency, governance with integration — are at high risk of discriminant-validity failure, a four-link chain of conceptually proximate constructs being a standard route to a measurement model that cannot be estimated as designed.

Alternative 2, parallel capabilities (accounting and governance both predicting integration, transparency omitted), would estimate cleanly but discards the paper's reason for existing. Removing transparency removes the accounting-specific mechanism, leaving a structure indistinguishable from established supply chain integration models with circular labels applied; an additive bundle says nothing about how information becomes actionable.

The adopted model relocates governance from the causal chain to the conversion condition, resolving all three problems at once: the indefensible path is removed, the chain shortens to three links with an interpretable indirect effect, and governance measured as accountability structure rather than joint activity has a sharper boundary against integration. More importantly, it converts the paper from describing a pathway to establishing a boundary condition — a claim that corrects an implicit consensus rather than confirming it.

## 4.4 Failure conditions

A theory earns its place by forbidding outcomes. Three are specified in advance. A null interaction alongside positive main effects would reduce the model to an additive bundle, and the theoretical contribution would fail. A strong direct path from transparency to value creation surviving controls would favour a simpler information-economics account. A null or reversed path from accounting capability to transparency would make accounting a consequence rather than an antecedent of visibility, inverting the framework. Each will be reported whichever way it falls.

Circular accounting capability and circular value creation are specified as reflective-formative higher-order constructs because their dimensions are not interchangeable. An actor may record physical volumes meticulously while never costing a loss, and may capture economic value from by-product sales while distributing none socially. Treating either as a single reflective construct would assume an interchangeability that both theory and the indicator literature contradict (Corona et al. 2019; Kristensen and Mosgaard 2020; Jerome et al. 2022), and higher-order reflective specification would invite the problems Lee and Cadogan (2013) identify. Specification follows Sarstedt et al. (2019): two-stage, with redundancy analysis against global single-item measures.

Circular accounting capability is distinguished from four neighbouring constructs, testably rather than by assertion. Environmental management accounting takes the firm's environmental impact as its object, and material flow cost accounting works within a process boundary; this construct takes the resource loop, spans exchange relationships and includes recovery valuation. Sustainability reporting is external, retrospective and applies to reporting entities; this is internal, prospective and applies to unincorporated enterprises. Eco-control is a control package; this is the calculative capacity that must exist before there is anything to control. Digital traceability capability, the construct with which it is most likely to be confused in a smart-production setting, concerns the technical capacity to capture and transmit item-level data; this construct concerns the capacity to convert such data into costed and valued decision objects, and the two are measured separately so that the distinction can be examined rather than assumed. Most fundamentally, conventional management accounting recognises purchased inputs and saleable outputs, whereas this construct recognises what the conventional system treats as nothing.

## 5.2 Empirical setting and unit of analysis

The proposed setting is a fragmented agro-industrial manufacturing network: the Malta (*Citrus sinensis*) processing network of the Indian Himalayan region, selected as a critical case rather than as the object of study. Food processing is a manufacturing sector in the standard industrial classification and one of the largest by employment in the economy concerned; the network's midstream and downstream tiers — juice and concentrate processors, peel and pomace valorisers, and shared Common Facility Centres — are production units whose recovery decisions determine whether material and its embodied carbon are retained or lost. Comparable settings have been studied in production research as manufacturing systems, including peach canning as a circular-intervention case (Paraskevopoulou et al. 2022), palm oil as an Industry 4.0-and-circularity case (Abdul-Hamid et al. 2022), and European food manufacturing as a production-loss case (Stapelbroek et al. 2022).

The setting exhibits the four scope conditions in unusually observable form. First, many dispersed small producers with no coordinating hierarchy. Second, substantial recoverable material currently lost, with official national estimates placing harvest and post-harvest losses in fruits at 6.02–15.05 per cent and the processed share of fruit output at 4.5 per cent (Ministry of Food Processing Industries 2025) — aggregates for national fruit output rather than for this network, cited only to establish that the recoverable fraction is material. Third, pronounced information asymmetry sustained by intermediary dependence, of the kind documented in comparable fragmented agri-industrial chains (Obonyo, Formentini, and Ndiritu 2025). Fourth, wide variation in what actors measure. Crucially, governance quality varies widely within this single network and regulatory environment, from informal spot transactions to formalised producer organisations — precisely the variance the focal moderation requires, and the variance that is compressed where circularity regulation supplies governance exogenously and near-uniformly.

The unit of analysis is the network actor enterprise, including unincorporated enterprises. Restricting it to incorporated entities would exclude most actors in any fragmented production network and render the scope condition unobservable, a restriction that has limited the multi-tier sustainability literature to the formal segment (Uddin, Ahmed, and Shahadat 2023). The key informant keeps or supervises records of quantities, costs and sales and participates in pricing and disposal decisions, confirmed by four screening items. Items are phrased as observable behaviours ("we write down how much fruit we reject or lose") rather than technical accounting knowledge: a smallholder who records rejected volumes and knows what they would have fetched is exercising circular accounting capability at low formality, and it is variance in that practice the model requires.

## 5.3 Sampling design

A composite sampling frame is assembled from horticulture, cooperative, market-licence and food-processing registers, institutional rosters, and grower-nominated referrals to unlicensed intermediaries. That last component is essential: informal intermediaries handle most volume but appear in no register, and excluding them would bias the sample toward the formal, better-governed segment where the predicted effects are strongest — the selection problem that most limits inference in multi-tier production research.

Sampling is stratified purposive with disproportionate allocation, supplemented by chain-referral for the informal midstream. Proportionate allocation would yield roughly eight processors, rendering the central recovery actors statistically invisible. The design is explicitly non-probability, so variable distributions are not generalisable, though structural relationships can still be estimated. The generalisability claim is theoretical — the mechanism travels — not statistical.

## 5.4 Sample size determination

Sample size is derived rather than asserted. ## 5.5 Instrument development and expert validation

Forty-six substantive items are administered on seven-point scales, consistent with the source instruments and preserving the variance that interaction detection requires. A visual ladder card and enumerator administration mitigate cognitive load, with pre-specified conversion to five points if pilot discrimination proves inadequate. Of the 46 items, 22 are adapted from validated scales, 11 adapt validated item structures to circular resource-flow content, 10 are newly developed and declared as such, and three constitute a validated marker. That roughly one item in five is new is neither concealable nor avoidable: no validated instrument exists for loss-recovery valuation, value-distribution transparency, responsibility allocation for losses, or social circular value — precisely the measurement gap the paper claims, and one consistent with the state of circular indicator development more generally (Pitkänen et al. 2023; Halter et al. 2025). The full instrument, with a source code against every item, is provided in Appendix A.

A two-task expert study ran over three rounds with an eight-member panel, following the procedure now standard for circular-practice instrument development (Mishra, Singh, and Govindan 2022). In task 1, all 46 items were rated for relevance, retention requiring an item-level index of at least 0.83 and a modified kappa of at least 0.74, with scale-level validity averaging at least 0.90 (Polit and Beck 2006; Polit, Beck, and Owen 2007). The first threshold deserves a note: 0.78 is frequently quoted but applies to panels of nine to ten raters, whereas six to eight require 0.83, meaning at least seven of eight must judge an item relevant. Task 2 was a blind item-placement task following Anderson and Gerbing (1991) and Moore and Benbasat (1991), placing items against the five construct definitions shown without labels. It tests the principal measurement risk directly: that governance cannot be distinguished from integration, or accounting capability from transparency. Two rules were pre-specified so that they could not be relaxed afterwards: items failing either placement criterion are revised and re-rated or dropped; and if more than 20 per cent of placements crossed either boundary, the corresponding hypothesis would be withdrawn. Rounds one and two administered both tasks in full, 368 ratings and 368 placements each; round three re-rated the revised items only. Returns were complete throughout. The sequence separates two explanations that a single round would have conflated. Round two changed the raters and left the items alone, so its gain is attributable to panel composition. Round three changed the items and left the raters alone, so its gain is attributable to revision. The seven items showing identical indices in both earlier rounds (CAC2, CGQ1, CGQ6, CVCI2, CIT3, IS1, MK3) were diagnosed as wording problems rather than panel artefacts; all seven moved once reworded, from 0.750 to between 0.875 and 1.000. The diagnosis was testable, and was tested.

Discriminant validity was established in round two. All five focal constructs met the placement criterion, including the two that failed in round one, and all four boundary checks passed. H6b is therefore well founded on discriminant grounds: the boundary whose failure would have made the focal interaction a construct interacting with itself leaks 6.2 per cent and 2.1 per cent against a 20 per cent tolerance.

Three qualifications carry forward. First, three of the fifteen failing items were not revised — IS2, IS3 and MK1 — and retain round-two ratings of 0.750 with a modified kappa of 0.719, below both thresholds. None is focal: two belong to the institutional-support control and one to the marker. The marker is a special case. It is the validated attitude-toward-the-colour-blue instrument, administered verbatim and chosen precisely because it is theoretically unrelated to every substantive construct (Miller and Simmering 2023), so a low relevance rating is what qualifies it rather than a defect; the remedy is to exclude it from the denominator rather than reword it, since rewording a published marker forfeits the warrant for using one. On the 43 items remaining, S-CVI/Ave is 0.913, so the criterion is met on all three denominators — 0.908, 0.913 and 0.926 — and the conclusion does not turn on which is chosen. IS2 and IS3 are double-barrelled and should be revised before fielding. Second, circular accounting capability remains below 0.90 at construct level, at 0.889. It is the model's antecedent, which makes this the least convenient place for a shortfall, and it is reported rather than absorbed into the focal average. Third, the placement task was not repeated after twelve items were reworded, so for those twelve the discriminant evidence describes superseded wording. Given leakage well inside tolerance the conclusion is unlikely to reverse, but it has not been re-tested; a confirmatory placement check is the cheapest remaining improvement to the instrument.

Dependent-variable items are framed as change over three seasons while predictors are current-state, giving temporal and format separation within a single administration at no cost to response burden. A subsample supplies three objective indicators transcribed from records — loss ratio, by-product sale and value, and realised price against the district modal price — permitting a convergent-validity check on the perceptual outcome measure.

# 6. Analytical strategy

Estimation uses partial least squares structural equation modelling, with covariance-based estimation as a pre-specified robustness check. The choice is not justified by sample size: at 520 responses covariance-based estimation is feasible, as Approach C establishes. It rests on measurement specification, since two constructs are reflective-formative, which covariance-based estimation accommodates only through identification constraints that would distort the theorised structure; on capabilities being composites; and on the presence of a latent interaction inside a serial chain.

The critique of composite-based estimation is engaged directly rather than by citing advocates only. Concerns about parameter bias under common-factor conditions and the absent global fit test (McIntosh, Edwards, and Antonakis 2014; Rönkkö et al. 2016) are met three ways. The constructs are theorised as composites, which is the condition under which composite estimation is appropriate rather than merely convenient (Hair, Sarstedt, and Ringle 2019; Guenther et al. 2023). The missing fit index is compensated by the standardised root mean square residual, out-of-sample prediction, and prediction-oriented comparison against the rejected specifications. And the model is re-estimated by covariance-based methods, with any divergence disclosed.

The protocol proceeds in eleven pre-registered steps: data preparation under pre-specified cleaning rules; measurement assessment with declared escalation contingencies; second-stage construction of the formative higher-order constructs; method-bias assessment; structural estimation; Johnson–Neyman analysis; conditional indirect effects; predictive assessment; multi-group analysis; and an eleven-check robustness battery. Bootstrapping uses 10,000 subsamples with percentile and bias-corrected intervals.

Two planned outputs deserve emphasis, because they are what convert the study from a directional claim into an actionable one. The Johnson–Neyman point identifies the governance level at which the transparency–integration relationship ceases to be significant, delivering a threshold that a policy instrument or a lead manufacturer can act on. The index of moderated mediation establishes whether the pathway from measurement to circular value creation exists at low governance quality at all; the theoretical claim is that it does not.

Common method bias is addressed procedurally — through anonymity, the change-versus-level framing separation, a scripted break and neutral item framing — and statistically through full collinearity assessment, a validated marker and measured latent marker correction. Harman's single-factor test is deliberately not used. Endogeneity is addressed through eight theoretically justified controls, of which digital traceability capability and institutional support are the principal rival explanations, together with a Gaussian copula assessment and estimation of the reversed model. The inclusion of digital traceability capability is central to the smart-production argument: if the accounting mechanism does not survive its inclusion, the correct conclusion is that circular accounting capability is a proxy for digital instrumentation rather than a distinct calculative capacity. Following Chenhall and Moers (2007), the design's limits are stated plainly: it establishes conditional association, not causation.

# 7. Discussion

Because the structural model is specified rather than estimated, this section discusses what the study has established — a construct, a validated instrument, a formally compared specification, and a set of falsifiable predictions — and relates each to the recent evidence base, identifying convergences and tensions that the eventual estimation will adjudicate.

## 7.1 What the validation establishes, and what it concedes

The three-round sequence establishes that circular accounting capability can be rated as a distinct, relevant and placeable construct by experts drawn from accounting, operations and circular-economy backgrounds, and that its most dangerous boundaries hold. The two pairs whose conflation would have invalidated the focal test — accounting capability with transparency, governance with integration — leaked 4.2 per cent and 2.1 per cent against a 20 per cent tolerance. That is a stronger discriminant result than the design anticipated, and it matters because the construct's novelty is precisely what invites the objection that it is a relabelling of environmental management accounting or of digital traceability.

Two concessions run the other way, and both are informative rather than merely regrettable. Circular accounting capability is the weakest focal scale at 0.889, below the 0.90 criterion, and it is the model's antecedent. The pattern of expert disagreement concentrated on the recovery-valuation dimension, which is the dimension for which no prior instrument exists and which the indicator literature identifies as least developed (Corona et al. 2019; Halter et al. 2025). The shortfall is therefore diagnostic of the field's state rather than of panel inattention, and it locates the priority for further item development precisely. Second, the round-one failure is attributable to a documented deviation from the pre-registered panel composition. Reporting a failed round rather than presenting only the round that succeeded is unusual, and it is what permits the causal separation in Section 5.5: composition explains the round-two gain, revision explains the round-three gain.

## 7.2 Convergences with recent evidence

Three convergences support the theorised mechanism.

First, the capability framing is now the dominant successful framing in the circular-and-digital production literature. Integrating circular economy with Industry 4.0 has been shown to require dynamic capability rather than technology adoption (Lu, Zhao, and Liu 2022); digital-enabled circular capabilities differ systematically by firm size, indicating that capability rather than technology carries the effect (Urueña et al. 2026); digital transformation's low-carbon effect is mediated by dynamic capabilities (Yang et al. 2023); and net-zero capabilities have been identified as the organisational condition for low-carbon operations in circular chains (Awan, Shamim, and Hannola 2025). The present study's contribution within that convergence is to identify *which* capability performs the sensing function and to give it an instrument.

Second, the conditional logic is corroborated in adjacent models even where it has not been named. Data-driven circular manufacturing maturity is explicitly structured so that data infrastructure is necessary but insufficient (Acerbi, Sassanelli, and Taisch 2024). Digital product passport implementation is analysed as an ecosystem-orchestration problem rather than a data problem (Agyemang et al. 2026). Digitalisation has been estimated as a moderator between supply chain efficiency and circular performance (Wang et al. 2026c), and relational governance effects on sustainability are themselves moderated by network complexity (Chowdhury et al. 2022). Decarbonisation's financial return has been shown to depend jointly on transparency and a second organisational property rather than on transparency alone (Wang et al. 2026a). Each is a different instantiation of the claim that an informational input requires a conversion condition, which is what H6b formalises and quantifies.

Third, the diagnosis of fragmentation is corroborated across settings that share the structural condition but nothing else. Information sharing in fragmented agri-industrial networks is constrained by intermediary structure rather than by technology (Obonyo, Formentini, and Ndiritu 2025). Sustainability requirements cascade through multi-tier production only where power sources permit (Marttinen and Kähkönen 2022) and where performance measurement systems exist at the supplier tier (Saunila, Ukko, and Jääskeläinen 2024). Circular networks that self-organise behave differently from planned ones (Massari, Nacchiero, and Giannoccaro 2025). Accountability failures propagate where responsibility is diffuse (Uddin, Ahmed, and Shahadat 2023). The scope condition is thus not a property of the chosen setting.

## 7.3 Tensions the estimation will adjudicate

Three tensions in the recent literature bear directly on the predictions, and the study is designed so that each is resolvable rather than restated.

The first concerns whether transparency is beneficial, harmful or inert. Evidence exists for all three: carbon transparency to consumers can damage the disclosing firm (Zhu, Duan, and Sarkis 2023); transparency plays a paradoxical role in the digitalisation–resilience relationship (Wang, Prajogo, and Gligor 2026b); restricted visibility can dominate full transparency in blockchain-enabled sharing (He et al. 2025); and disclosure without consequence changes nothing (Sodhi and Tang 2019). The conditional specification reconciles these results rather than adding to them: if the transparency effect is governance-contingent, then studies sampling weak-governance settings will find null or negative effects, studies sampling strong-governance settings will find positive effects, and neither is wrong. The Johnson–Neyman point is the quantity that would demonstrate this, and it is what a directional study cannot produce.

The second concerns whether digital instrumentation is sufficient. Policy has proceeded as though it were; reviews and firm-level studies suggest it is not (Chauhan, Parida, and Dhir 2022; Truant et al. 2024; Chung, Tan, and Yoshie 2025). Modelling digital traceability capability as a control rather than as the focal antecedent puts this question to a direct test: the accounting mechanism must survive the inclusion of the technology with which it is most likely to be confounded, or the paper's premise fails. That is a harder test than the literature has yet applied.

The third concerns the social dimension of circular value. Circular performance is overwhelmingly instrumented economically and environmentally, with social indicators least developed and least validated (Pitkänen et al. 2023; de Oliveira and Oliveira 2023; Blandino and Montagna 2025). The formative specification permits the dimensions to respond differently, and the theoretically interesting outcome is the asymmetric one: if economic and environmental value respond to integration while social value does not, the finding speaks to who captures the value that circularity releases in a network where bargaining power is unequal — a distributional question that material-efficiency metrics cannot pose.

# 8. Theoretical contributions

**To the dynamic capabilities view.** Dynamic capabilities theory, including its applications to accounting and to circularity, locates capability and its returns inside the firm. Where the value-creating reconfiguration crosses organisational boundaries — which is the standing condition of net-zero targets that reach beyond the factory gate — returns are relationally contingent, depending on a governance arrangement the focal actor does not control. The contribution is not that capabilities require complementary assets, which Teece (2007) states, but the identification of *which* asset substitutes for hierarchy under fragmentation, and a design in which its absence nullifies the capability's effect. This sharpens the capability accounts now dominant in circular and digital production research (Lu, Zhao, and Liu 2022; Urueña et al. 2026; Awan, Shamim, and Hannola 2025) by specifying the boundary within which they hold.

**To operations and production management.** The paper introduces a calculative layer between digital instrumentation and circular outcomes, and thereby offers an explanation for the field's unstable findings on digitalisation and sustainability. The proposition is that the smart-production literature has been estimating a three-stage mechanism as a one-stage one. Data must become a costed and valued object, and the valued object must become an allocated responsibility, before material re-routing occurs. Positioning circular accounting capability as the first conversion and governance as the second gives production research a specification in which digital adoption, calculative capacity and accountability architecture are distinguishable and separately estimable.

**To circular economy research.** Circular performance is predominantly measured as material or energy efficiency. This paper specifies circular value creation as a formative higher-order construct spanning economic, environmental and social value, and examines whether the mechanism differs across dimensions. It also relocates the field's central problem: Corvellec, Stowell, and Johansson (2022) criticise circular-economy research for attributing agency to "the circular economy" rather than to actors and mechanisms, and a moderated mediation model estimated at actor level with a governance contingency is a direct answer to that criticism.

**To net-zero and carbon measurement research.** Product and organisational carbon accounting has advanced substantially in allocating emissions within and to manufactured products (He, Qian, and Li 2023; Xiang et al. 2024; Patro et al. 2025). The contribution here is complementary and prior: it addresses the flows that fall outside every allocation base because no entity recognises them. An inter-organisational material loss is an unrecognised embodied-emission loss, and the reason it is unrecognised is not technical but calculative and jurisdictional. Framing Scope 3 as an accountability problem rather than a data problem is a distinct theoretical position, and it is testable in the form given here.

**To sustainability accounting.** Two claims are advanced. Accounting is the sensing apparatus of circularity: a discarded fraction becomes an economic object only when quantified and valued. And accounting's effect on circular value creation is entirely indirect and conditional — no direct path is specified, and the analysis tests whether the indirect path exists at all where governance is weak. That is a more precise proposition than "accounting supports circularity," and it specifies when investment in circular measurement is worthwhile and when it is not.

**To governance and accountability research.** The literature repeatedly asserts that transparency requires verification, consequence or participation to matter. This paper specifies that assertion as a conditional relationship, provides the means to quantify the threshold at which it binds, and names the diagnostic core of fragmented circularity: resource loss is nobody's account.

**Methodologically.** The paper supplies a replicable instrument spanning an entire fragmented production network rather than a single tier, with newly developed items identified, three rounds of validation reported in full including a failed round, a blind item-placement test of the boundaries whose failure would invalidate the focal hypothesis, a formal comparison of specification alternatives with the attenuation arithmetic made explicit, and a sample size derived against the interaction rather than the largest path. Each of these is a transferable practice for capability-and-performance research in production and operations management, where under-powered interaction tests and unreported specification searches remain common.

# 9. Managerial and practitioner implications

The implications below follow from the model's logic and from the validated instrument; they are stated as decision rules that become quantified once the structural estimates are available.

**For manufacturing firms and production managers.** Begin with calculation, not with disclosure. The instrument in Appendix A can be used as a diagnostic: an enterprise that scores highly on physical measurement but poorly on loss costing and recovery valuation has a data system and not a calculative capability, and will not convert instrumentation into recovery decisions. Practically, this means extending the cost system to recognise rejected, off-grade and residual fractions as objects with quantities, costs and forgone values, and doing so at the handover points where material leaves the firm's control — the locations that plant-bounded material flow cost accounting does not reach.

**For operations and supply chain managers.** Treat transparency initiatives as incomplete until responsibility is allocated. Before investing in traceability or passport infrastructure, establish who is answerable for the loss the infrastructure will reveal. The model predicts that visibility without an accountability relation produces compliance data and no material change, and the paradoxical transparency findings in the recent literature are consistent with that prediction. The sequencing rule is: allocate responsibility for losses, agree how recovered value is shared, then instrument.

**For sustainability officers and ESG functions.** Scope 3 inventories built from average emission factors applied to purchased quantities are structurally blind to inter-organisational loss, because the loss is not a purchase. Where a firm's abatement claim depends on upstream or midstream recovery, the inventory must be reconciled against physical loss measurement at the tiers concerned, and the capacity to produce that measurement is unevenly distributed across the supplier base. Investing in supplier calculative capability is therefore an abatement activity, not an administrative one.

**For Industry 4.0 and digital transformation practitioners.** The model implies a specific sequencing for smart production investment in circular applications. Sensing and traceability infrastructure raise the ceiling on what can be calculated; they do not raise calculation. A digital twin or passport programme that is not paired with a costing and valuation layer, and with an accountability architecture among the actors whose flows it makes visible, should be expected to deliver auditability without abatement. Where budgets are constrained, the marginal return is higher on the calculative and governance layers than on additional sensing.

**For lead manufacturers governing dispersed supplier networks.** The Johnson–Neyman threshold, once estimated, identifies the governance level below which supplier transparency programmes should not be expected to change material flows. Below that threshold the appropriate intervention is governance construction — clear loss responsibility, joint verification, participatory decision-making on how recovered value is shared, and enforceable commitments that make recovery investment safe — rather than additional information requirements. Above it, information requirements are productive.

**For producer organisations, cooperatives and shared-facility operators.** These bodies are the practical governance vehicles in fragmented networks. The model identifies their circular function precisely: they can hold responsibility for aggregate loss, verify what members report, and make the sharing of recovered value negotiable, which is what enables members to invest in recovery. Their circular value is therefore governance provision, not only market access, and their performance should be measured accordingly.

**For technology and service providers.** The commercial implication is that the addressable gap is the calculative layer. Traceability platforms that terminate at item-level data leave their customers unable to answer the question that drives a recovery decision — what is this loss worth, and what would recovery cost — and platforms that close that gap, and that support multi-party responsibility allocation, address a need the current market under-serves.

# 10. Sustainability implications

The study's sustainability contribution is infrastructural. It concerns the conditions under which circular and low-carbon intentions become measurable, attributable and therefore actionable in production networks, and it is set out below against the objectives the Special Issue addresses.

**Net-zero manufacturing and carbon neutrality.** The material fraction that leaves a production network unrecorded carries embodied emissions that leave with it. Because the loss is recognised by no entity, its emissions appear in no inventory, and abatement targets are consequently set on a base that omits them. The framework identifies the mechanism by which those quantities become computable — actor-level calculative capability — and the condition under which computing them changes behaviour — allocated accountability. On this account, credible net-zero manufacturing requires calculative capacity distributed across the network rather than concentrated in the reporting entity, which is a different implementation programme from the one that current disclosure regimes imply.

**Circular economy and waste reduction.** The study reframes waste reduction as a calculative and jurisdictional problem before it is a technological one. Where a discarded fraction has no quantity, no cost and no owner, no recovery decision can be made about it, however available the recovery technology. Making the fraction visible, valued and owed is the precondition for slowing, closing and narrowing loops in a network that no single actor controls.

**Resource efficiency and material productivity.** Because circular accounting capability is specified to include recovery valuation, it supports comparison between recovering a fraction and the alternative use of the same capacity. That comparison is what turns resource efficiency from an aspiration into an investment appraisal, and it is precisely what plant-bounded costing cannot support for flows that cross the boundary.

**Sustainable industrial ecosystems.** The framework treats the network, not the firm, as the unit at which circular value is created, and identifies governance as the ecosystem property that determines whether it is created. This gives industrial-symbiosis and shared-facility arrangements a testable performance mechanism: their contribution is the accountability architecture they supply, and their circular output should be attributed to that.

**Social sustainability and just transition.** The formative specification of circular value creation includes a social dimension covering the distribution of recovered value, and the model permits the dimensions to respond differently. This makes distributional failure an observable outcome rather than an assumption. In fragmented networks the actors best placed to observe losses are frequently those with least bargaining power, and a circular transition that raises aggregate recovery while concentrating its returns is a possibility the design can detect and most circularity metrics cannot.

**Sustainable Development Goals.** The contribution maps to SDG 12 most directly, through the measurement of material loss and recovery that Target 12.3 and Target 12.5 presuppose; to SDG 9, through the resource efficiency and upgrading of small-scale industrial enterprises under Target 9.4; to SDG 13, through the emissions embodied in unrecorded loss; and to SDG 8 and SDG 2 in the specific setting, through the incomes and food loss associated with the recovered fraction. The mapping is deliberately conservative: the study contributes measurement and accountability infrastructure for these targets, not abatement itself.

# 11. Policy implications

The policy implication concerns sequencing, and it is the study's sharpest practical claim.

**Measurement mandates and transparency requirements.** Instruments from sustainability reporting directives to digital product passports rest on the premise that visibility changes behaviour. If governance is the conversion mechanism, then where responsibility for loss is unallocated such requirements will generate compliance without circular value. The recommendation is not to weaken transparency requirements but to pair them with responsibility allocation and enforcement capacity, and to sequence them: allocate, then require. A transparency mandate imposed on a network below the governance threshold should be expected to produce reporting cost without abatement, and evaluating such instruments on disclosure completeness rather than on material outcomes will conceal that.

**Industrial decarbonisation policy.** Scope 3 obligations should be understood as obligations to construct quantities that do not currently exist, distributed across actors of very unequal calculative capacity. Policy that assumes the numbers are available and merely unreported will produce estimates built from average factors, which are structurally insensitive to precisely the loss reduction the policy seeks. Supporting calculative capability at the small and medium-sized supplier tier is therefore an abatement instrument, and a low-cost one relative to sensing infrastructure.

**Circular economy policy and extended producer responsibility.** Extended producer responsibility is, in the terms of this model, an instrument that allocates accountability for a flow that would otherwise be nobody's account (Tong et al. 2024; Jin et al. 2024). The framework predicts that its effect on circular outcomes will be strongest where informational capability already exists and weakest where it does not, which implies that responsibility allocation and measurement support are complements rather than alternatives. Extending such schemes to fragmented, informal-intermediary segments requires attention to who can actually compute the quantities on which liability is assessed.

**Carbon border adjustment and trade instruments.** Border adjustment mechanisms transmit carbon obligations to producers in jurisdictions where entity-level calculative capacity is thinnest, and where the affected producers are frequently unincorporated. The analysis implies that the binding constraint on compliance will be calculative rather than technological, and that transitional support should be directed accordingly. Analytical work on cross-regional carbon pricing (Hua et al. 2024) can be extended with this constraint made explicit.

**Industrial regulators and standard setters.** Two specific recommendations follow. First, loss and recovery categories should be codified so that they can be recognised consistently across actors in a network; codifiability is what permits cross-boundary coordination, and it is a public good that no individual actor will supply. Second, digital product passport and traceability standards should specify not only the data carried but the responsibility relations the data implies, since an ecosystem-orchestration problem cannot be solved by a data schema alone (Agyemang et al. 2026; Gosling et al. 2026).

**Manufacturing associations, cooperatives and sustainability agencies.** Intermediary bodies are the practical route to both conversions in fragmented networks. They can supply codified loss categories and shared measurement services at a scale individual small enterprises cannot, and they can hold the accountability relations that make transparency productive. Programme design should treat these as a single intervention rather than as separate measurement and governance initiatives.

**A note on where the premise can be tested.** Where circularity regulation supplies governance exogenously and near-uniformly, the conditional relationship is difficult to observe for want of variance. A mature disclosure regime is therefore a poor place to test the premise on which its own instruments rest, which is a substantive reason for studying fragmented settings rather than a concession about them.

# 12. Limitations and future research

The limitations are stated in order of severity, and each is paired with the research that would address it.

The most consequential limitation is that the structural model is specified but not estimated. The empirical content of this paper is the construct development and the three-round expert validation; every structural claim is a prediction with a stated failure condition. The immediate priority is estimation on primary data from a single production season against the pre-registered protocol in Section 6, with all three failure conditions reported whichever way they fall.

The cross-sectional design supports conditional association rather than causation (Chenhall and Moers 2007). A longitudinal panel across seasons is the principal design improvement, and it would additionally permit the reverse-causal question — whether transparency slowly restructures governance, as the structural reading of Gereffi, Humphrey, and Sturgeon (2005) implies over longer horizons — to be examined rather than assumed away.

Measurement is single-informant and largely perceptual. The objective-indicator subsample mitigates but does not eliminate this, and because enterprises able to produce records are those with higher accounting capability, that check is itself conditional. A dyadic design with matched producer–buyer pairs would be superior for the governance constructs but is not feasible where intermediary relationships are informal and often deliberately opaque — which is the condition under study.

Sampling is non-probability, so variable distributions are not generalisable and the generalisability claim is theoretical rather than statistical.

Ten items are newly developed, and after three rounds of validation content validity meets the scale-level criterion with three qualifications carrying forward, as Section 5.5 reports: two control items and one marker item were never revised and remain below threshold; circular accounting capability, the model's antecedent, is the weakest focal scale at 0.889; and the placement task was not repeated after twelve items were reworded. A confirmatory placement round is the cheapest remaining improvement.

The instrument measures material and monetary flows, not emissions. This is the most important substantive limitation for the net-zero agenda and it is deliberate: adding carbon items after three completed validation rounds would invalidate the reported content-validity statistics. The environmental dimension of circular value creation therefore proxies avoided loss rather than measured avoided emissions. Extending the instrument with explicitly carbon-denominated items — embodied emissions per tonne recovered, avoided emissions attributable to redirected flows — and revalidating is the single highest-value instrument development the framework invites, and it would connect the model directly to the product-level carbon accounting literature (He, Qian, and Li 2023; Xiang et al. 2024).

The social dimension rests on the least developed indicator literature (Pitkänen et al. 2023; Blandino and Montagna 2025), and its measurement should be regarded as provisional.

Digital traceability capability is modelled as a control rather than as a structural variable. This is the right conservative choice for a first test, but it forecloses the question of how the technical and calculative layers interact. A three-way specification in which digital capability, calculative capability and governance jointly determine circular reconfiguration is the natural extension, and it would give the socio-technical argument in Section 3.4 a full test rather than a partial one.

Beyond these, six research directions follow from the framework.

*Artificial intelligence and automated calculation.* If the binding constraint is calculative rather than sensing capacity, then machine learning applied to loss estimation and recovery valuation — inferring costed loss from process data without a formal cost system — could substitute for capability that small enterprises lack. Whether automated calculation reproduces the behavioural effects of developed calculative capability, or merely its outputs, is an open and consequential question (Jackson et al. 2024; Zheng et al. 2025; Wu 2026).

*Digital twins and simulated recovery appraisal.* Digital twins have been implemented for performance improvement in process industries including food manufacturing (Maheshwari et al. 2022), and they offer a route to appraising recovery options before committing assets. Coupling a twin to the recovery-valuation dimension of circular accounting capability would test whether simulation reduces the safeguarding costs that governance otherwise addresses.

*Industrial internet-of-things instrumentation at handover points.* The model locates the critical unmeasured flows at inter-organisational boundaries. Instrumentation designed for those points, rather than for the interior of processes, is an underdeveloped design problem with direct implications for Scope 3 measurement.

*Industry 5.0 and the human dimension.* The human-centric, resilient and sustainable framing of Industry 5.0 (Leng et al. 2026; Karmaker et al. 2023; de Mendonça Santos et al. 2026) intersects the present model at the social dimension of circular value and at the informant-level nature of calculative capability. Who in a small enterprise performs the calculation, and how automation reallocates that work, is a socio-technical question the framework can accommodate.

*Circular and carbon accounting across other fragmented production systems.* The model applies unchanged to textile recovery, construction and demolition waste, and electronics, and fails only where hierarchy performs the coordination. Replication in a non-agricultural fragmented network, and in a vertically integrated setting where the conditional effect should attenuate toward zero, would test the scope condition directly rather than asserting it.

*Green innovation and sustainable manufacturing ecosystems.* Whether circular accounting capability contributes to green innovation as well as to recovery — that is, whether valuing losses changes what firms design rather than only what they retain — connects the framework to the eco-innovation and green-innovation literatures (Triguero and Córcoles 2025; Shahzad, Ben Zaied, and Zhang 2026; Mehmood et al. 2024) and would extend it from recovery to prevention.

# 13. Conclusion

Net-zero manufacturing is a vision expressed in quantities, and its realisation depends on two conversions that the vision itself does not supply. Someone must compute the quantities, and someone must be answerable for them.

This paper has argued that circularity in fragmented production networks is substantially a calculative and an accountability problem, and that the two are related in a testable way. Accounting is what makes a resource loop visible: until a discarded fraction is quantified and valued it is not an opportunity but an absence, and no volume of sensor data alters that. Yet visibility is not self-executing. In a firm, hierarchy converts information into action; in a fragmented network there is no hierarchy, and what converts information into action is governance — responsibility for what is lost, monitoring of what was agreed, participation by those who share the gains, and enforceability of the commitments that justify investment in recovery assets.

The paper contributes an operationalisation of circular accounting capability at the inter-organisational level, an instrument validated across three rounds and reported in full including a failed round, a formal rejection of the specifications a reader would otherwise propose, and a pre-registrable protocol powered against the interaction rather than the largest path. It makes the central argument falsifiable: the pathway from measurement through transparency to circular value creation is predicted to exist only above a threshold of governance quality, and the analysis that would locate that threshold is specified in advance. If the prediction fails, the account reduces to an additive capability bundle and should be abandoned; that condition is stated before the data are collected.

The contribution of production and operations research to industrial decarbonisation will not be exhausted by extending sensing or disclosure. What is measured must also be owed by someone to someone, with consequence. In fragmented manufacturing networks, accounting produces the visibility that circularity requires; governance determines whether that visibility does any work.
