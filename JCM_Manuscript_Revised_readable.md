Satisfaction as the Conduit: How User Engagement and Experience Create Brand Value in the Metaverse

Abstract

Purpose: This study investigates the drivers of brand success in the metaverse by empirically testing a configurational model connecting user engagement (UE), user experience (UX), and brand satisfaction (BSAT), grounded in Flow Theory and Self-Determination Theory (SDT).

Design/methodology/approach: A mixed-methods research design is employed. Partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) validates the measurement model, while fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) addresses causal complexity. The study draws on data collected from 312 active metaverse users in India, a strategically important emerging market.

Findings: Stand-alone engagement or experience is insufficient for brand success. Rather, specific configurations—particularly brand satisfaction situated alongside either user experience or user engagement—are required for favourable outcomes. The fully triadic configuration (BSAT × UX × UE) yields the highest solution consistency, demonstrating equifinality in metaverse branding.

Practical implications: The configurational logic provides brand managers and strategists with actionable guidance for designing identity-affirming, community-embedded virtual experiences that sustain engagement and competitive differentiation in the metaverse.

Originality/value: This paper extends Flow Theory and SDT into co-creative, technology-mediated brand ecosystems. By using fsQCA, it reveals the complex, equifinal pathways to brand success that traditional, linear engagement models often overlook.

Keywords: Metaverse; User engagement; User experience; Brand satisfaction; Brand success; fsQCA

1. Introduction

The metaverse is an immersive virtual environment that uses augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) technologies, enabling users to interact with one another and with virtual objects while experiencing digital information in the real world (Arya et al., 2024a; Zallio and Clarkson, 2022). It is reshaping how brands connect with consumers, as traditional marketing gives way to digital experiences that deliver tangible value (Hennig-Thurau et al., 2023; Anderson et al., 2024).

Unlike conventional digital channels, it offers multisensory brand experiences that bridge physical and digital realms and link individual interactions with broader social networks, requiring brands to adapt if they are to remain competitive (Kopalle et al., 2024; Marvi et al., 2024; Bilgihan et al., 2024; Hwang and Seo, 2025). Yet scholarship on integrating the metaverse into business strategy remains sparse (Eggenschwiler et al., 2024). Nike’s Nikeland on Roblox, with its interactive mini-games and rewards, illustrates how virtual environments can redefine marketing (Kumar and Shankar, 2024; Kozinets, 2023).

Branding in this setting differs from website or app branding in four respects that motivate a configurational approach: environments are persistent, shared three-dimensional spaces; users act through avatars, so identity and embodiment fuse with consumption; interaction is socially synchronous, creating real-time co-presence with other users and with brands; and value is frequently co-created. Brand outcomes therefore emerge from combinations of these attributes rather than from any single one, which is why this study adopts a configurational lens and asks which recipes of engagement, experience and satisfaction jointly produce brand success.

Emotional connection is central in this setting. Emotional attachment correlates with stronger brand relationships (Bilgihan et al., 2024; Arya et al., 2022), and immersive, personalised interactions build satisfaction, trust and brand identification (Shamim et al., 2025; Vo et al., 2025). Brands can also transcend physical constraints to reach global audiences, with personalisation strengthening brand perceptions and satisfaction (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Chandra et al., 2022).

The study therefore asks how immersive, avatar-mediated interactions shape the configurations of user engagement, user experience and brand satisfaction that lead to brand success. Two sub-questions follow: which combinations are sufficient for high brand success, and what role does brand satisfaction play in linking engagement and experience to success?

The study makes three contributions. First, rather than re-confirming that engagement, experience and satisfaction matter, it specifies how immersive, avatar-based environments reconfigure them, arguing that equifinality—the existence of multiple sufficient pathways to success—is especially pronounced in the metaverse. Second, it brings Flow Theory and SDT together as complementary lenses that explain why immersive interactions sustain engagement and shape satisfaction. Third, through fsQCA it identifies the combinations of engagement, experience and satisfaction associated with high brand success, offering managers configurational guidance.

2. Literature review

2.1 Immersive environment

Firms can enhance metaverse experiences through three components: immersiveness, sociability and ecological faithfulness (Arya et al., 2024a). Immersiveness—the mental absorption and sensory engagement often termed presence—strengthens emotional engagement and, through it, user satisfaction and brand trust (Shamim et al., 2025; Daassi and Debbabi, 2021; Bilgihan et al., 2024). Sociability, the quality of social interaction, enables collaboration, community building and emotional bonding, with social presence lifting enjoyment and advocacy (Hennig-Thurau et al., 2023; Osborne, 2024), while ecological faithfulness, the authenticity of virtual environments, builds trust among environmentally conscious consumers (Dwivedi et al., 2023).

Immersive storytelling, gamification and virtual product launches represent a substantive growth avenue and can build loyalty (Bilgihan et al., 2024; Blazquez, 2024; Lee and Shen, 2024; Rather et al., 2022). Poorly executed design, by contrast, may trigger frustration, overstimulation or escapism, which is why psychologically safe, user-centred strategies matter (Lavoie et al., 2021; Duong et al., 2025).

2.2 Conceptualisation of user engagement in the metaverse

2.2 Conceptualization of User Engagement in The Metaverse

No single definition has settled the matter, yet most accounts describe the metaverse as a three-dimensional, shared virtual world powered by AR/VR that allows people to move seamlessly between physical and virtual domains for work, interaction and socialisation (Buhalis et al., 2023; Koohang et al., 2023; Kozinets, 2023). Building on early engagement theory, O’Brien and Toms (2010) developed a validated, multifaceted engagement scale, while the mobile user engagement model explains engagement through motivation, perceived value and satisfaction (Soltani Nejad et al., 2024; İlkan et al., 2023).

2.3 Impact of user behaviours on brand engagement in virtual environments

User behaviours—purchasing NFTs, interacting socially, attending virtual events and creating content—sit at the centre of brand engagement in immersive ecosystems, shaping perceptions, building emotional bonds and driving loyalty (Shamim et al., 2025; Bilgihan et al., 2024; Lee and Shen, 2024).

Branded NFTs enable digital self-expression, exclusivity and identity construction, and owning unique avatars, wearables and collectibles strengthens emotional attachment (Xie et al., 2023; Simonetti et al., 2025). Scarcity and perceived investment value further lift brand attitudes, amplified by social-comparison dynamics (Wang et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024).

Social interaction reinforces engagement: brand communities build trust, knowledge-sharing and purchase intention, and avatar-based interactivity deepens belonging and loyalty (Agnihotri et al., 2024; Kumar and Shankar, 2024; Payal et al., 2024).

Virtual events and gamified rewards build trust, satisfaction and loyalty, especially among Gen Z (Habachi et al., 2024), and participatory content creation deepens ownership and advocacy (Bilgihan et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024).

2.4 Impact of user engagement and experience on brand success in virtual environments

User engagement and experience are major drivers of brand success. Wiertz and De Ruyter (2007) showed that contributions to firm-hosted online communities enhance brand success, with knowledge-sharing accounting for much of the engagement effect.

Brodie et al. (2011) conceptualise engagement as active participation in brand experiences, while purchase intentions in virtual communities often serve social and self-identity needs, framing engagement as a route to loyalty.

3. Theoretical foundation

3.1 Flow theory

Flow Theory (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990) offers a robust framework for interpreting engagement in the metaverse. Flow is a state of deep immersion and focused attention characterised by high intrinsic motivation and enjoyment, and it is particularly consequential where engagement determines brand success. Recent studies extend it to virtual and augmented reality: perceived enjoyment enhances flow and, in turn, satisfaction and continued use in game-based metaverses (Jo and Lee, 2025).

Evidence continues to link flow with virtual brand experiences. In live streaming, authenticity, interactivity and entertainment enhance flow, with interactivity the most influential (Wu et al., 2024), while metaverse adoption in emerging markets is shaped by technological factors (Liang et al., 2024). In VR, flow lengthens subjective time (Rutrecht et al., 2021) and influences participative and sharing behaviours (Lo and Lai, 2023). The survey therefore incorporated flow elements—clear goals, immediate feedback and challenge–skill balance—on which participants reported for their metaverse interactions.

3.2 Self-Determination Theory (SDT)

Self-Determination Theory (SDT) holds that motivation and well-being are maximised when three innate needs are satisfied: autonomy (volitional choice), competence (feeling effective) and relatedness (feeling connected) (Deci and Ryan, 2000; Ryan and Deci, 2017). In digital environments, the intrinsic motivation to explore, master and connect drives engagement further.

The two theories are complementary. Flow captures the moment-to-moment immersion that arises when challenge–skill balance aligns and goals and feedback are clear, while autonomy facilitates exploration, competence fosters mastery and relatedness sustains social interaction through avatars (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990); SDT explains why users enter and remain in flow. Together they constitute the immersion and need satisfaction underlying engagement, satisfaction and loyalty.

3.3 Constructs and hypotheses

Four constructs are examined. User engagement (UE) is the cognitive, emotional and behavioural involvement a user has with a brand during metaverse interactions. User experience (UX) is the perceived quality of that interaction—immersion, usability, enjoyment and presence. Brand satisfaction (BSAT) is the user’s overall evaluation of the brand arising from metaverse encounters. Brand success (BSUC) covers brand-relevant outcomes: continuance intention, purchase intention, advocacy and word of mouth. The SDT needs and flow dimensions are treated as underlying psychological mechanisms rather than as separately measured constructs.

Read together, Flow Theory and SDT imply that immersive, need-satisfying interactions raise engagement and experience, which in turn shape satisfaction and brand outcomes. Higher user engagement is therefore expected to be positively associated with brand success (H1), and higher user experience quality to be positively associated with brand success (H2). Because satisfaction summarises cumulative evaluations, brand satisfaction is expected to mediate the engagement/experience–success relationships (H3). Consistent with a configurational view, high brand success may arise from multiple sufficient configurations rather than from any single necessary condition (P1). 4. Methodology

4.1 Fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis and its application to the study

Jia et al. (2022) advocate a mixed-method approach to studying complex causal configurations in branding: fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA). Positioned between exploratory and hypothesis-testing strategies, fsQCA demonstrates how combinations of conditions—present or absent—jointly produce an outcome and reveals equifinality (Schneider and Wagemann, 2012), and it remains viable in small-N research (Ragin, 2008).

The procedure comprises six steps, summarised in Figure 3: case selection, identification of causal conditions, calibration of membership scores, truth-table construction, logical minimisation, and consistency and coverage checks. Boolean minimisation derives minimal sufficient combinations, revealing causal complexity that single-variable effects would not detect.

4.2 Identification and selection of variables

Variable selection followed a structured three-stage process. First, a literature review identified consistently validated variables—satisfaction, interaction frequency, perceived brand impact, emotional connection and personalisation—as core indicators (Cadet and Chainay, 2020; Lu and Lau, 2025).

Second, interviews and focus groups provided a finer reading of involvement, satisfaction drivers and brand-success perceptions, and the variables were refined accordingly. Third, surveys captured UE, UX, BSAT and BSUC; immersiveness was measured via presence scales, sociability via social and co-presence measures, and ecological faithfulness via authenticity measures (Cadet and Chainay, 2020; Poeschl and Doering, 2015; Lu and Lau, 2025).

4.3 Measures

All constructs were measured on seven-point Likert scales anchored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). User engagement was adapted from O’Brien and Toms (2010); user experience from O’Brien and Toms (2010), Basu et al. (2024) and Cadet and Chainay (2020); brand satisfaction from Fornell et al. (1996); and brand success was developed for this study drawing on Mathwick et al. (2008). The engagement and experience scales are ten-item adapted subsets, and full item wordings are available from the authors on request.

4.4 Platforms studied and metaverse activities

Three prominent platforms were studied—Roblox, Decentraland and The Sandbox—selected for their adoption, functionality and capacity to host brand interactions. Roblox, known for user-generated content and social gaming, hosts branded experiences such as Nike’s Nikeland (Bilgihan et al., 2024); Decentraland, built on blockchain, allows users to buy virtual land, trade NFTs and attend community events (Brouard, 2024); and The Sandbox supports voxel-based creation and monetisation, and is used by brands such as Adidas (Brandes and Dölp, 2025).

Across the platforms, four activity types were considered: trading digital assets, attending virtual events, avatar-based social interaction, and designing digital assets or experiences (Lee and Shen, 2024; Hennig-Thurau et al., 2023).

4.5 Data collection

Data were collected using stratified random sampling. From a frame of adults (18+) active on at least one platform with prior brand-related metaverse experience, 600 eligible users were invited at random, stratified by primary activity (gaming, NFT trading, social interaction, content creation and virtual events). Of these, 312 provided complete, consenting responses—a 52% response rate—with balanced representation across strata, measured on validated UE, UX, BSAT and BSUC scales.

India offers a theoretically and empirically justified context. It is one of the world’s fastest-growing metaverse markets (USD 2 billion in 2023, projected to grow at a robust CAGR through 2030), with a large, digitally native youth population (median age 28), rapid 5G expansion and high mobile-first penetration (Verma, 2024; Statista, 2025; Dwivedi et al., 2023). Indian consumers adopt avatar-based self-expression and NFT ownership readily, and studying this underexplored, non-Western market extends the theoretical reach and generalisability of the findings (Payal et al., 2024).

5.3 Data analysis using fsQCA

5.3.1 Data-driven results from fsQCA

To analyse UE, UX, BSAT and BSUC, raw responses were calibrated (Ragin, 2008) into fuzzy-set membership scores, each expressing the degree to which a case belongs to a condition and to the outcome.

Calibration used three anchors: the 95th percentile for full membership (0.95), the 50th as the crossover point (0.50) and the 5th for full non-membership (0.05), which constrains scores to the 0–1 interval (Ragin, 2008; Douglas et al., 2020). This shared scale permits direct comparison of the calibrated UE, UX, BSAT and BSUC values.

Table 3 reports the intermediate solution for BSUC = f(BSAT, UX, UE). Three two-condition configurations—BSAT × UX, BSAT × UE and UX × UE—are each sufficient for high brand success and together account for approximately 32% of the outcome, at an overall solution consistency of 0.756.

In the model (Figure 2), UX, UE and BSAT act as the antecedent conditions for BSUC. The subset–superset analysis establishes which combinations are jointly sufficient for high brand success rather than estimating a single directional path, which anchors the conceptual model in the empirical configurations.

The configurational results demonstrate equifinality: several distinct paths lead to the same outcome (Ragin, 2008). No single factor—BSAT, UX or UE—is necessary in isolation; it is their interaction that drives brand outcomes in immersive digital environments.

Each sufficient path carries a distinct substantive meaning. BSAT × UX delivers brand success even without strong engagement, because satisfied users with positive immersive experiences form lasting relationships (Bilgihan et al., 2024). BSAT × UE combines satisfaction with active participation, lifting loyalty and advocacy. UX × UE indicates that immersive, interactive environments yield favourable outcomes even without reported satisfaction, which supports Flow Theory (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990).

The subset–superset analysis broadens this picture. The triadic path (BSAT × UX × UE) records the highest consistency and combined coverage: satisfied, engaged and immersed users raise brand success to its peak, consistent with service-dominant logic and value co-creation. The remaining paths afford strategic latitude, since satisfaction combined with either experience or engagement is sufficient, allowing brands to tailor strategies to their strengths, while UX × UE, despite lower consistency, supports immersive participatory experiences (Shin, 2019).

5.3.2 Necessity analysis

Necessity analysis establishes whether a condition must be present for the outcome to occur; a condition qualifies as necessary only if its consistency exceeds 0.90 (Schneider and Wagemann, 2012). As Table 3 shows, none of the single conditions approaches that threshold. High brand success therefore depends on combinations rather than on any individually essential condition, which motivates the sufficiency analysis.

5.3.3 Sufficiency analysis

The sufficiency analysis identifies the configurations that are jointly sufficient. UE, UX and BSAT were entered into the truth table with consistency and frequency thresholds applied (Ragin, 2008; Schneider and Wagemann, 2012). As Table 3 reports, brand satisfaction appears in every high-consistency sufficient path, whereas engagement and experience contribute in combination rather than in isolation—a pattern consistent with prior evidence on engagement–loyalty links (Jeong and Kim, 2019) and social-media effects on customer equity (Ebrahim, 2020).

User engagement in this setting is multifaceted—asset trading, event attendance, social interaction and content creation—and reflects psychological, social and experiential motivations that the UE and UX constructs capture rather than platform-specific variables.

5.4 Hypothesis evaluation

fsQCA tests sufficiency rather than net linear effects, so the hypotheses are evaluated configurationally against Table 3. Consistent with the necessity result, user engagement and user experience are not sufficient in isolation; they support brand success only in combination, which supports H1 and H2 within sufficient configurations. Brand satisfaction appears in every high-consistency path and records the highest single-condition consistency, indicating the central, mediation-consistent role posited in H3. The presence of three sufficient configurations rather than one necessary condition supports the equifinality proposition (P1). A formal bootstrap mediation test would provide a useful complement.

6. Discussion

The fsQCA findings clarify how combinations of UE, UX and BSAT contribute to brand success in the metaverse and delineate the principal pathways to favourable outcomes.

The BSAT × UX configuration is substantively important: satisfied users who perceive a high-quality immersive experience become loyal advocates, consistent with prior research linking satisfaction, experience and emotional connection (Thaichon et al., 2022; Bilgihan et al., 2024). Brands should therefore prioritise seamless immersive experiences while addressing ethical concerns such as data-privacy risks and excessive use.

BSAT × UE was similarly strong, echoing established engagement–equity links (Jeong and Kim, 2019): brands should promote gamified and co-creation activities that deepen engagement without compromising satisfaction. UX × UE, although less consistent, indicates that engagement is best achieved where experience quality is high, even when satisfaction is not reported, in line with the challenge–skill balance (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990; Shin, 2019).

Individual conditions recorded lower consistency than their combinations, which argues for holistic strategies: the triadic path achieved the highest consistency, reinforcing Flow Theory’s premise that optimal engagement arises from a balance between challenge and skill in immersive environments (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990).

7. Implications

7.1 Theoretical contributions

This research integrates SDT and Flow Theory within immersive, digitally mediated environments defined by persistent communities, avatar-based identity and interactive interfaces, clarifying how psychological needs and experiential states jointly shape engagement, satisfaction and brand outcomes. Earlier applications addressed traditional or gamified settings; metaverse interactions instead blend intrinsic motivation and experiential absorption in a distinctive way, which carries both theories into co-created domains.

Within SDT, autonomy, competence and relatedness assume distinctive forms: autonomy through customisable avatars and digital-asset ownership, competence through skill-based challenges, and relatedness through persistent social interaction in branded communities (Bilgihan et al., 2024). These are advanced as conceptual mechanisms underlying engagement and experience rather than as separately measured effects.

The study extends Flow Theory by proposing that experiential immersion depends on matching task challenge to user skill, tuned by digital affordances and interaction design (Zhong and Hamouda, 2024), with gamified missions and event-based interactions acting as flow facilitators. Deep immersive engagement can therefore yield favourable outcomes even when satisfaction is not the dominant condition.

Taken together, the confluence of need satisfaction and experiential immersion is associated with engagement, satisfaction and brand performance, extending SDT and Flow Theory into co-created digital domains while keeping tested associations distinct from theoretical interpretation.

7.2 Managerial implications

User experience recurs across the configurations associated with success, so designing engaging, interactive and personalised metaverse experiences is central to sustaining engagement and long-term brand success.

Brands should build immersive experiences tied to users’ virtual identities: customisable avatars and interactive environments allow users to express who they are and strengthen presence, reinforcing the emotional bond. Where a brand’s virtual presence matches users’ self-concept, tailored experiences deepen engagement, loyalty and advocacy (Thaichon et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2023).

Frequently discussed features—NFT interoperability, smart contracts and rarity dynamics—may strengthen exclusivity and trust, but they were not measured here and are therefore presented as future avenues rather than findings.

The results also indicate synergy between satisfaction and engagement (Jeong and Kim, 2019): managers should develop gamified, co-creation and reward opportunities that deepen involvement, because a user’s sense of ownership reinforces attachment and long-term loyalty.

Immersive offerings must accordingly be emotionally resonant, consistent and aligned with users’ values and identities; functional or visually appealing assets are no longer sufficient, and brands delivering high-quality interactive experiences build lasting engagement, loyalty and competitive advantage.

8. Limitations and future scope

The study carries several limitations. The sample of 312 active, experienced users ensures relevant expertise but may tilt results toward highly engaged participants and underrepresent casual or novice users, which limits generalisability; a more diverse, representative sampling frame is needed.

Inclusion and exclusion decisions may have omitted relevant factors, and the absence of a control group limits causal attribution. While fsQCA handles complex causal analysis well, its small-N, qualitative orientation reduces robustness relative to larger quantitative models and limits methodological triangulation.

The cross-sectional design cannot track how engagement and experience evolve; longitudinal studies are needed to establish whether engagement is sustained. Ethical questions—privacy, addiction, cyberbullying and exploitation—remain underexplored, and future work should develop safeguards for user well-being.

Several large technology firms have curtailed their metaverse investments, so the contribution is framed conservatively. The findings speak most directly to brands active on large, metaverse-like platforms—game-based worlds such as Roblox and Fortnite, and social virtual reality—and to the design of future immersive environments, since the psychological mechanisms of engagement, experience and satisfaction are likely to persist across successive technological forms.
